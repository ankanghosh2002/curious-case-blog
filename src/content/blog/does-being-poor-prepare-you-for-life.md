It is often said that children who are raised in poor families are better prepared to deal with the practical problems of life than children raised by rich families. I firmly believe that parents from economically humble backgrounds can, in many ways, raise children who are better prepared to tackle the problems of adult life. But perhaps the real question is not whether being poor makes someone better, but whether **struggle teaches lessons that money sometimes hides.**

![does-being-poor-prepare-you-for-life.jpeg](/uploads/does-being-poor-prepare-you-for-life.jpeg)

Families that do not have a large amount of money experience the harsh realities of life and society at a very early stage. Their children get to experience all kinds of challenges, struggles, and mishaps because of the lack of monetary support their family can provide. Those children who, despite all the odds that life has thrown at them, still succeed and overcome the ups and downs of life get to experience nearly all kinds of problems that adulthood has to offer. They learn early that things break, money runs out, plans fail, and sometimes you simply have to figure things out yourself. Hence, I strongly agree with this thinking. An 18-year-old boy who cycles 11 km everyday to reach his school understands the value and worth of money differently from a kid who has their own car and a driver or a college bus to drop them off at college. The first boy knows what it means to be tired before the day has even properly begun; he truly knows what hunger is when he brings boiled vegetables and a piece of bread or a packet of biscuits for lunch. The second boy might never have had to think about it, since he has always been given everything on a platter. When he is hungry, he gets the privilege of ordering food from a fancy restaurant rather than just sitting in dismay and eating every bite of bread crumbs on his own, all alone.

There is something strangely educational about hearing the words, **"We can't afford it."** When you hear them often enough, you begin to understand that wanting something does not mean you automatically get it. You learn to wait, calculate, compromise, repair, reuse, and some times simply accept that you cannot have what you want. These are small lessons, but adulthood is basically made up of thousands of small problems disguised as ordinary days. For some, it's a tire leak, and they have to walk 3 miles to are pair shop, while there are some whose sole problem is that their driver is unavailable for the day and they have to take a cab to commute. Rich kids are provided with all their needs and desires from the beginning of their lives. Hence, they don't necessarily get to experience the hard work, struggle, and perseverance their families had to go through to provide that lifestyle. This can make children born into affluent families less prepared for society and the crises that come with adult life. For example, a 20-year-old rich girl won't think twice before spending ₹5,000 at a nightclub, whereas for some families, that could be a whole month's grocery expense. Here, the rich girl doesn't necessarily realize the value of having money or how to tackle adult problems without relying on money. It isn't necessarily her fault either. If nobody ever taught her what ₹5,000 means to another family, why would she know?

**Adulthood doesn't care how much money your parents had.** Your car can break down. Your job application can get rejected. Your relationship can fall apart. Your bank account can suddenly look terrifying. Someone you love can get sick. Life can throw a problem at you that cannot simply be solved by calling your parents or swiping a card. And when that happens, the person who has spent their childhood solving small problems may already know something the other person is learning for the first time sometimes nobody is coming to save you.

I still believe that children raised by economically humble families can be better prepared to deal with adult life because they often encounter practical struggles much earlier in life. They learn the value of money, patience, perseverance, compromise, and independence from situations rather than textbooks. But perhaps being poor is not the advantage. **Learning how to struggle, fail, wait, adapt, and solve problems is.**

Maybe the real advantage isn't growing up poor.

Maybe it's growing up **without being rescued from every problem.**
